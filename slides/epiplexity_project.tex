\begin{frame}{Epiplexity: information accessible to a bounded learner}\vspace{-2mm}\small\begin{center}\small\textbf{Epiplexity with lightweight learning algorithms}\end{center}
Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity do not explicitly restrict the computational resources of the observer. Finzi et al.~\citep{finzi2026} introduce \emph{epiplexity} as the structural information in a distribution that can be extracted by a computationally bounded observer.
For a $T$-time probabilistic model $P$ with program length $|P|$, define
\[
P^* = \arg\min_{P\in\mathcal P_T}
\left\{|P| + \E_{X}\left[-\log P(X)\right]\right\}.
\]
The corresponding decomposition is
\[
\underbrace{\MDL_T(X)}_{\text{total time-bounded information}}
=
\underbrace{|P^*|}_{S_T(X)\;\text{-- epiplexity}}
+
\underbrace{\E[-\log P^*(X)]}_{H_T(X)\;\text{-- time-bounded entropy}}.
\]
Thus, epiplexity is not simply a property of the dataset: it is defined relative to a computational budget and an admissible class of observers. In practice, neural training procedures provide tractable approximations to this program optimization.
\vspace{1mm}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Key reference:} Finzi et al., ``From Entropy to Epiplexity'', 2026. The paper's definition is compute-bounded; practical estimators approximate the optimal program through training trajectories.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we want to test: the estimators, not the architectures}
\small
The original empirical study demonstrates epiplexity with neural observers and sweeps over model size and training duration, including synthetic cellular automata and natural-data experiments \citep{finzi2026}.
Our question is not which architecture is better. We treat MLP, CNN and DiT as three different \emph{setups} in which the same estimators are evaluated, and ask whether the estimated structural information remains comparable when the setup, the manipulations applied inside it, and the compute budget change:
\[
X \xrightarrow{\ \text{setup } a\ } P_T,
\qquad
a \in \{\text{MLP},\ \text{CNN},\ \text{DiT}+\text{sampler}\}.
\]
Two things are varied at once and have to be told apart:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{manipulations} applied inside a setup (data ordering, pixel permutation, sampler schedule, budget);
\item the \emph{observer family} itself --- hypothesis class and inductive bias.
\end{itemize}
The target is a statement of the form: this part of the estimate is a property of the data, and this part is produced by the setup in which we happened to measure it.
\end{frame}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Estimators: one learning trajectory, several codes}
\footnotesize
We will evaluate several measures on the same training trajectories rather than introducing separate experimental setups for each metric.
\\
\textbf{Prequential coding.} The description length is accumulated from predictions made by successive checkpoints before the corresponding training information is available. In the implementation of Finzi et al., the model-code contribution is estimated from the area of the training-loss curve above the final loss \citep{finzi2026}:
\[
K_{\mathrm{preq}} \;\approx\; \sum_t \ell_t\,\Delta t
\quad\text{relative to the final-loss baseline.}
\]
\textbf{Requential coding.} Qiu et al.~\citep{qiu2026} replace coding of the exact observed samples by coding synthetic samples on which teacher and student disagree. The central quantity is the cumulative divergence
\[
K_{\mathrm{req}} \;\propto\; \sum_t \KL\!\left(P_t^{\mathrm{teacher}}\,\middle\|\,P_t^{\mathrm{student}}\right).
\]
This removes much of the cost associated with the entropy of the particular dataset sequence.
\textbf{EDL.} Donoway et al.~\citep{donoway2026} define Excess Description Length as the gap between sequential training-code length and the residual code length under the final predictor. It is closely related to learning dynamics, but is a measure for a specified learning algorithm rather than the optimization over all admissible programs defining epiplexity.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Experimental design: setups and the knobs attached to them}
\footnotesize
The dataset and the compute budget are fixed. What varies is the setup, its scale, and the manipulation applied inside it.
\vspace{1mm}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
& MLP & CNN & DiT \\
\midrule
Scale & $10^3$--$10^6$ & $10^3$--$10^6$ & small $\rightarrow$ medium \\
Prior & weakest & locality & generative, iterative \\
Optimizer & SGD / Adam(W) & SGD / Adam(W) & Adam(W) \\
Extra knob & input order & pixel permutation & sampler, \#steps \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The primary synthetic benchmark is elementary cellular automata, following the controlled setting of Finzi et al.~\citep{finzi2026}: their local transition rules give tasks with different degrees of learnable structure while keeping the data-generating mechanism explicit.
Each setup carries its own manipulation, and the three are deliberately of a different kind:
\begin{itemize}
\item MLP: no built-in spatial prior, so the order in which inputs are presented is itself part of the observer;
\item CNN: a fixed random permutation of pixels is applied to image data. Labels are unchanged, but the spatial arrangement the architecture exploits is destroyed;
\item DiT: the sampler is part of the observer. Different schedules use different numbers of model evaluations and follow different trajectories through the learned score field.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Validation and readout}
\footnotesize
For every task $d$, setup $a$, compute budget $T$ and seed $s$ we record the learning trajectory and derive
\[
S_{\mathrm{preq}},\qquad S_{\mathrm{req}},\qquad \mathrm{EDL}.
\]
Three checks are applied to the resulting table:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Agreement between estimators.} The orderings they assign to the same tasks are compared by Spearman correlation and Kendall's $\tau$, both within and across setups.
\item \textbf{Setup dependence.} For each pair of setups we report
\[
\Delta_{ab}(d,T) = S(d,a,T) - S(d,b,T),
\qquad a,b \in \{\text{MLP},\ \text{CNN},\ \text{DiT}\}.
\]
A large $\Delta$ with a stable task ordering means the estimator is partly re-measuring the setup rather than the data.
\item \textbf{Seed variance.} Reported explicitly and compared against the spread across setups; final test loss and sample efficiency serve as external baselines.
\end{itemize}
The DiT setup is the sharpest case, because there the observer is not a single function but a compute schedule: we can ask whether this compute-bounded view separates sampling strategies from one another when final sample quality does not.
\vspace{1mm}
The deliverable is a map of where these estimators are setup-invariant and where they are not --- not a ranking of architectures.
\end{frame}
