\begin{frame}{Epiplexity: information accessible to a bounded learner}\vspace{-2mm}\small\begin{center}\small\textbf{Epiplexity with lightweight learning algorithms}\end{center}

Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity do not explicitly restrict the computational resources of the observer. Finzi et al.~\citep{finzi2026} introduce \emph{epiplexity} as the structural information in a distribution that can be extracted by a computationally bounded observer.

For a $T$-time probabilistic model $P$ with program length $|P|$, define
\[
P^* = \arg\min_{P\in\mathcal P_T}
\left\{|P| + \E_{X}\left[-\log P(X)\right]\right\}.
\]
The corresponding decomposition is
\[
\underbrace{\MDL_T(X)}_{\text{total time-bounded information}}
=
\underbrace{|P^*|}_{S_T(X)\;\text{-- epiplexity}}
+
\underbrace{\E[-\log P^*(X)]}_{H_T(X)\;\text{-- time-bounded entropy}}.
\]

Thus, epiplexity is not simply a property of the dataset: it is defined relative to a computational budget and an admissible class of observers. In practice, neural training procedures provide tractable approximations to this program optimization.

\vspace{1mm}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Key reference:} Finzi et al., ``From Entropy to Epiplexity'', 2026. The paper's definition is compute-bounded; practical estimators approximate the optimal program through training trajectories.
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What we want to test: the observer may matter}
\small

The original empirical study demonstrates epiplexity using neural observers and computational sweeps over model size and training duration, including synthetic cellular automata and natural-data experiments \citep{finzi2026}.

Our question is narrower and experimentally controllable:

\begin{center}
\large
\textbf{How does the estimated structural information change when the observer is replaced by a lightweight architecture?}
\end{center}

We will keep the data-generating process fixed and vary the observer
\[
X \xrightarrow{\text{learning algorithm}} P_T
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\text{MLP}\;\;\text{vs.}\;\;\text{CNN}.
\]

The comparison is motivated by the compute-bounded definition itself: changing the hypothesis class and its inductive bias changes the set of programs that can be practically realized within a fixed budget. CNNs additionally encode locality, whereas MLPs provide a substantially weaker architectural prior.

This lets us separate two questions that are often conflated: \emph{how much structure is present} and \emph{how much structure is accessible to a particular learner}.
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Estimators: one learning trajectory, several codes}
\footnotesize

We will evaluate several measures on the same training trajectories rather than introducing separate experimental setups for each metric.

\textbf{Prequential coding.} The description length is accumulated from predictions made by successive checkpoints before the corresponding training information is available. In the implementation of Finzi et al., the model-code contribution is estimated from the area of the training-loss curve above the final loss \citep{finzi2026}:
\[
K_{\mathrm{preq}} \;\approx\; \sum_t \ell_t\,\Delta t
\quad\text{relative to the final-loss baseline.}
\]

\textbf{Requential coding.} Qiu et al.~\citep{qiu2026} replace coding of the exact observed samples by coding synthetic samples on which teacher and student disagree. The central quantity is the cumulative divergence
\[
K_{\mathrm{req}} \;\propto\; \sum_t \KL\!\left(P_t^{\mathrm{teacher}}\,\middle\|\,P_t^{\mathrm{student}}\right).
\]
This removes much of the cost associated with the entropy of the particular dataset sequence.

\textbf{EDL.} Donoway et al.~\citep{donoway2026} define Excess Description Length as the gap between sequential training-code length and the residual code length under the final predictor. It is closely related to learning dynamics, but is a measure for a specified learning algorithm rather than the optimization over all admissible programs defining epiplexity.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Experimental design: lightweight observers}
\small

We will use a controlled grid: the dataset and compute budget are fixed, while the observer architecture and its scale are varied.

\vspace{1mm}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & MLP & CNN \\
\midrule
Parameters & $10^3$--$10^6$ & $10^3$--$10^6$ \\
Capacity & shallow $\rightarrow$ deep & shallow $\rightarrow$ deep \\
Optimizer & SGD / Adam(W) & SGD / Adam(W) \\
Seeds & \multicolumn{2}{c}{5--10 per configuration} \\
Compute & \multicolumn{2}{c}{matched budgets} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The primary synthetic benchmark will be elementary cellular automata, following the controlled setting of Finzi et al.~\citep{finzi2026}. Their local transition rules provide tasks with different degrees of learnable structure while keeping the data-generating mechanism explicit.

For image data, we will additionally apply a fixed random permutation of pixels. Labels are unchanged, but the spatial arrangement exploited by a CNN is destroyed. The comparison therefore probes the interaction between data structure and architectural inductive bias.

\footnotesize
The main control is matched compute rather than matched epochs.
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Validation and potential application}
\footnotesize

For every task $d$, observer $a$, compute budget $T$, and seed $s$, we record the learning trajectory and derive
\[
S_{\mathrm{preq}},\qquad S_{\mathrm{req}},\qquad \mathrm{EDL}.
\]

Different estimators will be compared by the ordering they assign to the same tasks, using Spearman correlation and Kendall's $\tau$. Observer dependence will be quantified by
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{arch}}(d,T)
=
S(d,\mathrm{CNN},T)-S(d,\mathrm{MLP},T).
\]
Seed-to-seed variation will be reported explicitly; final test loss and sample efficiency will serve as external baselines.

\vspace{2mm}
\textbf{Potential application: DiT with a sampler.}

A natural extension is to study Diffusion Transformers together with their sampling procedures. The relevant computational object becomes
\[
\text{noise}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{DiT}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{sampler}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{generated distribution}.
\]

Different samplers use different numbers of model evaluations and follow different trajectories through the learned score field. After validating the methodology on lightweight observers, we can ask whether this compute-bounded perspective distinguishes sampling strategies beyond final sample quality alone.
\end{frame}
