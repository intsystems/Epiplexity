\section{Introduction}

A time-series foundation model forecasts series from different domains without
fine-tuning~\citep{ansari2025chronos2}. Training can use synthetic data alongside real
series. The generator defines a prior distribution over stochastic processes, and a
model trained on series from this prior approximates the associated Bayesian
forecast~\citep{muller2022}. Choosing the generator remains an open problem. At equal
training budgets, eleven generators produce models whose accuracy can differ by up to
a factor of two. Their ranking depends on the architecture, and there is no established
way to select a generator in advance~\citep{nagpal2026}.

Epiplexity~\citep{finzi2026} measures the amount of learnable structure that a
compute-limited model extracts from data. A practical estimate is the area under the
training loss curve and above the final loss. The authors hypothesize that this
structure determines transfer to new tasks better than the in-domain loss does.
Experiments with language models support the hypothesis on five domains~\citep{su2026}.
We ask what determines performance on real time series after synthetic pretraining,
whether generator epiplexity explains it, and which function of the generator and model
could guide generator selection. Such a function would let us compare generators
without evaluating them on the benchmark.

\paragraph{Benchmark and metrics.} The benchmark contains real series from energy,
transportation, sales, web traffic, and medicine. For each series, the model sees the
history and predicts the future over a specified horizon. GIFT-Eval provides these tasks
across dozens of datasets and frequencies. We use 55 short-horizon tasks, with horizons
of 6 to 60 points. The model predicts a distribution of future values, represented in
practice by 100 sample paths. CRPS measures the discrepancy between the forecast
distribution and the observed value. For a point forecast it reduces to absolute error.
MASE measures the scaled absolute error of the forecast median. Lower values are better.
We report both metrics relative to the error of a seasonal-naive forecast that repeats
the last season, so a ratio below one indicates an improvement. Across tasks we use
the geometric mean of the ratios or the mean model rank. Across the eleven generators
we measure rank association with Spearman correlation and bootstrap confidence intervals
by resampling generators.

Synthetic time-series generators fall into four groups. Parametric families include
ARIMA, ETS, and GARCH. Basic signal compositions combine trends, seasonality, steps,
and waves. Stochastic processes include Gaussian processes, SDEs, and fBm, while
nonlinear systems include chaotic attractors and NARMA.
KernelSynth~\citep{ansari2024} uses a Gaussian process whose kernel is a random
composition of base kernels. In the Chronos ablation, the best synthetic share is
about one-tenth of the corpus. Training on synthetic data alone performs worse than
training with real data. TempoPFN~\citep{moroshan2025} trains exclusively on ten
generators. Its performance drops most when the regime-switching
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process is removed (Figure~\ref{fig:tempo}).
Chronos-2~\citep{ansari2025chronos2} learns multivariate capabilities from synthetic
data by imposing multivariate structure on univariate series.

Mix, Don't Pick~\citep{nagpal2026} compares eleven generator families (KernelSynth,
ETS, Waveform, SDE, StepFunction, TSI, ARIMA, fBm, GARCH, Chaotic, TimeSynth). The
authors train two architectures from scratch at equal budgets and evaluate them on
GIFT-Eval without fine-tuning. The best and worst generators differ by a factor of
1.5 to 2 in CRPS. The ranking depends on architecture. ETS ranks second for
Moirai-Small and last for Chronos-T5-Mini. An equally weighted mixture of all generators
matches or exceeds the best single generator, and combining real and synthetic data
beats either corpus alone. The paper leaves the rule for selecting a generator open.

\subsection{The model as a Bayesian predictor}

The prior $p(\theta)$ defines a distribution over processes. A process $\theta$
generates a series, and a window has context $c$ and future segment $f$.
The Bayesian forecast under this prior is
\[
p(f\mid c)=\int p(f\mid c,\theta)\,p(\theta\mid c)\,d\theta
\]
and its expected log loss, the minimum attainable for series from this prior, is
the conditional entropy $H(f\mid c)$. A model $q_\phi(f\mid c)$ trained with log loss
on windows from the prior minimizes
\[
\mathbb{E}_{c}\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(\cdot\mid c)\,\|\,q_\phi(\cdot\mid c)\bigr)+H(f\mid c),
\]
and converges to the Bayesian forecast given sufficient capacity and
data~\citep{muller2022}. Training amortizes Bayesian inference so that prediction
requires only a forward pass. This gives two consequences for synthetic data.
A model trained on a generator approximates that generator's Bayesian forecast.
Its performance on real series depends on how well this Bayesian forecast transfers
and how closely the model approximates it. Second, entropy $H(f\mid c)$ bounds the
training loss from below. For nearly deterministic series with a continuous density,
this bound can be arbitrarily low. Comparing generators therefore requires a common
resolution or measuring loss above a known lower bound.

The error of a learned predictor decomposes into three parts~\citep{jeon2024}.
Let $H_{M,T}$ be a corpus of $M$ tasks of length $T$ drawn from a prior with parameter
$\psi$, and let $\theta_m$ and $D_m$ be the parameter and data for task $m$.
\[
\mathcal{L}_{M,T}
=\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{H}(H_{M,T}\mid\theta_{1:M})}{MT}}_{\text{irreducible}}
+\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{I}(H_{M,T};\psi)}{MT}}_{\text{meta-learning}}
+\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{I}(D_m;\theta_m\mid\psi)}{T}}_{\text{within-task learning}}.
\]
The first term is process noise. The third is the cost of inferring the series
parameters from context under a known prior. The second is the cost of learning the
prior from the corpus, equal to their mutual information. This is the error of an
ideal Bayesian learner seeing the corpus for the first time. Accumulating its excess
over the entropy across $N$ windows gives a lower bound on the exact-baseline area
for any model.
\[
A_{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_i-N\,H(f\mid c)\;\ge\;
A_{\mathrm{B}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{E}\,\ell^{\mathrm{B}}_i-H(f\mid c)\bigr),
\]
Here $\ell_i$ is the model's loss on window $i$ before its update, and
$\ell^{\mathrm{B}}_i$ is the Bayesian learner's loss after the preceding $i-1$
windows. For a prior with $p$ continuous parameters, the Clarke--Barron
asymptotic~\citep{clarke1990} gives $A_{\mathrm{B}}=\tfrac{p}{2}\log N+O(1)$.
The Bayesian contribution grows logarithmically. The excess above it is the cost
of learning the forecasting algorithm and the precision of the process.

For priors with a known Bayesian forecast, we can compute the bound and the
Bayesian learner exactly. The posterior predictive distribution of an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process or a Gaussian process with a known kernel is Gaussian
and has a closed form. For Gaussian families with finitely many parameters,
Monte Carlo estimates $A_{\mathrm{B}}$. These oracle generators separate the
network's area into the unavoidable Bayesian part and the network's excess.

\subsection{Epiplexity}

\paragraph{Definition.} Among programs that can evaluate probabilities and draw
samples within time $T$, take the one with the shortest two-part
code~\citep{finzi2026}.
\[
\begin{gathered}
\mathrm{P}^\star=\arg\min_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_T}\Bigl(|\mathrm{P}|+\mathbb{E}\log\tfrac{1}{P(X)}\Bigr),\\
S_T(X)=|\mathrm{P}^\star|,\qquad H_T(X)=\mathbb{E}\log\tfrac{1}{P^\star(X)}.
\end{gathered}
\]
Epiplexity $S_T$ is the description length of the best model within budget $T$, the
structural part of the information. Time-bounded entropy $H_T$ is the residual loss
of that model, the random part. Uniform noise has $S_T$ bounded by a constant. A
periodic pattern has both parts $O(1)$. A cryptographic pseudorandom number generator
has almost maximal entropy and no accessible structure. As the budget grows,
$S_T+H_T$ is nonincreasing. Usually $S_T$ rises and $H_T$ falls. Restricting the
program class to models attainable by a particular architecture and optimizer
makes epiplexity model-dependent.

\paragraph{Estimating epiplexity from the learning curve.} At step $i$, the
current model encodes example $Z_i$ in $\log 1/P_i(Z_i)$ nats and then trains on it.
The sum encodes both the data and the final weights. With the final weights given,
the data cost $\sum_i\log 1/P_M(Z_i)$. Their difference estimates the model length:
\[
A=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_i-N\,\Lfin,
\]
the area under the learning curve and above the final loss. It subtracts the final
model's loss from the prequential code of Blier and Ollivier~\citep{blier2018}.
Measure each loss before updating on the example, using a single pass of novel
examples. The estimate is heuristic and can rank datasets from existing learning
curves. Requential coding~\citep{qiu2026} gives a rigorous version that encodes
weights through a teacher and produces codes several times shorter, generally with
the same dataset order. Section~\ref{sec:estimators} discusses the estimators.

\paragraph{Transfer.} \citet{finzi2026} hypothesize that transfer depends on how
much reusable structure the model captures in its weights. Corpora with equal
in-domain loss can transfer differently. The paper offers indirect evidence.
Chess records ordered board$\to$moves yield higher epiplexity and better position
evaluation, and adaptive domain selection with ADO~\citep{jiang2025} raises both
epiplexity and accuracy. The authors distinguish the amount of structure from its
relevance to a particular task. Structure accounted for a small share of the
information in natural data, highest in text and lowest in images.

\paragraph{Direct test on language data.} \citet{su2026} trained one model for one
epoch on each of the five largest Pile domains. They estimated epiplexity from the
loss curve and measured performance on seven out-of-domain tasks. Pearson
correlation was $0.88$ and Spearman correlation $0.90$. Weight norm had no predictive
value as a control. Smaller domains were excluded because they would require
repeated examples. EpiSelect uses this relationship to weight domains by epiplexity
gains predicted online from scaling laws. EpiGen trains a synthetic generator with
a reward based on the student's loss drop. Our design substitutes eleven generators
for five domains and GIFT-Eval for language tasks. The generators can produce
unlimited data.

\paragraph{Counterevidence.} \citet{li2026} construct two corpora. One has abundant
structure irrelevant to the target task and the other has less structure, but more
of it is relevant. Overall structure ranks the first higher, while accuracy on the
shifted task favors the second in two of three seeds. For cellular automata,
downstream performance is nonmonotonic in rule complexity~\citep{zhang2024}.
The best complexity for synthetic data also depends on the target domain~\citep{lee2026}.
Total structure and task-relevant structure can diverge. The area depends on the
training recipe, including learning rate, warmup, batch size, and
schedule~\citep{bornschein2022}.

\paragraph{Baselines for data selection.} DataDecide~\citep{magnusson2025} ranks
corpora using one small model size. It predicts the better corpus at a large size
in around 80\,\% of pairs, matching or exceeding extrapolation from scaling laws.
ADO~\citep{jiang2025} reweights domains during training using scaling laws fit to
the loss curves.

\section{Epiplexity estimators}\label{sec:estimators}

Epiplexity minimizes over programs, which makes the exact quantity uncomputable.
Each estimate chooses a model class, a training recipe, and a baseline. The
following estimators illustrate what can be measured and which theoretical
questions remain open.

\paragraph{Area above held-out loss.} Use $A=\sum_i\ell_i-N\Lfin$, where
$\Lfin$ is the loss of the final averaged weights on fresh windows from the
same generator. This takes one epoch. Error in $\Lfin$ is multiplied by $N$.
Nearly deterministic series can drive loss very low and yield large areas.
The area also depends on the training recipe. Other baselines include late
training loss, first-pass loss, and the minimum across checkpoints. These
choices target the same quantity with different biases.

\paragraph{Area above the exact lower bound.} For oracle priors,
$A_H=\sum_i\ell_i-N\,H(f\mid c)=A+N(\Lfin-H)$. This decomposes into the
Bayesian part $A_{\mathrm{B}}$, of order $\log N$, and the network's excess.
For generators with nearly deterministic series, the excess includes the
cost of learning the process precision, or how narrow its density is.
That cost increases with resolution. A hypothesis is to define epiplexity
as a function of resolution $S(\sigma)$, where $\sigma$ is the shared
precision at which loss is measured. Compare generators at the same $\sigma$.
The curve $S(\sigma)$ describes structure across scales. Where no oracle
is available, fit $L(n)=E+a\,n^{-\alpha}$ to estimate the floor and test
the fit on generators with a known floor.

\paragraph{Requential coding with an oracle teacher.} In \citet{qiu2026},
a student learns from samples drawn from a teacher. The teacher can be
a saved model or a sequence of models. The leading term of the expected
code length is $\sum_i\mathrm{KL}(P^{\mathrm{t}}_i\,\|\,P^{\mathrm{s}}_i)$
plus coding overhead.
When the Bayesian forecast for the prior is known, use $p(f\mid c)$ as the
teacher. The leading term is then $\sum_i\mathbb{E}_c\,
\mathrm{KL}(p(\cdot\mid c)\,\|\,q_i(\cdot\mid c))$, equal in expectation
to $A_H$ for the student's trajectory. The coder and decoder must share
the contexts or account for them in the code length.
For Gaussian and Ornstein--Uhlenbeck processes the teacher is Gaussian.
KL has a closed form for a Gaussian student. For a mixture student, estimate
it by Monte Carlo using teacher samples. How does the oracle-teacher code
compare with a moving-teacher code on the same priors, and which ranking
agrees with the area?

\paragraph{Compute-optimal coding.} For each budget $T$, find the shortest
two-part code on a grid of model and dataset sizes. Its parts estimate
$S_T$ and $H_T$~\citep{finzi2026}. This requires a range of model sizes.
How does each generator's $S_T$ change with $T$, and at what budget does
the ranking stabilize?

\paragraph{Training recipe and units.} The definition also minimizes over
training recipes, so sensitivity to learning rate and schedule is estimation
error. One option is a shared recipe. Another is to tune the recipe for
each generator and use its minimum. Assess the stability of generator
rankings across recipes. Areas can be compared within one model because
different models may measure loss on different objects.

\section{Alternatives to epiplexity}\label{sec:alternatives}

We seek a function of the generator and model that predicts how the model
will perform on real series after synthetic pretraining. Epiplexity is one
candidate. The function should depend on the model because generator
rankings differ across architectures. It should be insensitive to increasing
precision on deterministic series, use no benchmark, and cost at most as
much as a full run.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,labelindent=0pt,labelwidth=0pt,labelsep=0pt,style=nextline,itemsep=2pt]
\item[Final loss and loss drop.] $\Lfin$ and $L_{\mathrm{start}}-\Lfin$
  reflect generator noise and precision. In loss units they are incomparable
  between generators as measures of structure.
\item[Measures without training.] Gzip compressibility, spectral entropy,
  and diversity of time-series features provide inexpensive, model-independent
  baselines. Their independence rules out explaining rankings that vary by architecture.
\item[External model loss.] Evaluate the loss or CRPS of a strong model
  trained on real series on the generator's series. This estimates proximity
  to real data without additional training.
\item[Distribution distance.] Compare generated and real windows in feature
  space or in a trained model's embedding space. MMD and a two-sample
  classifier directly measure domain similarity.
\item[Conditional epiplexity.] Measure the area on real series while
  fine-tuning a model pretrained on the generator. Smaller area suggests
  that synthetic data supplied more relevant structure. This reflects both
  amount and relevance of structure and requires real data.
\item[Predictive $\mathcal{V}$-information.] Measure how access to context
  improves forecasts of real series for a class of models trained on the
  generator. The predictor family defines this quantity.
\item[Information in the corpus about the prior.] The meta-learning term
  $\mathrm{I}(H;\psi)$, the number of processes distinguishable from a
  window, and the Fisher information in a window about the process are
  computable for oracle priors. They measure prior structure independently
  of the network.
\item[Short runs and small models.] Evaluate early checkpoints or small
  models on part of the benchmark~\citep{magnusson2025}. This is inexpensive
  and requires the benchmark. An additional measure is the rate of early
  loss decrease. In our runs, the area for the earliest windows correlates
  positively with CRPS, so faster starts yield worse models.
\item[Contribution to a mixture.] Measure how removing a generator changes
  mixture performance. This directly informs corpus composition and supplies
  more observations for correlation analysis.
\item[Epiplexity at fixed resolution.] Use $S(\sigma)$ from
  Section~\ref{sec:estimators}, the same area measured at a common precision
  for all generators.
\end{description}

\section{Experiment}

We trained Moirai-Small from scratch on each of the eleven Mix, Don't Pick
generators at an equal budget of forty million windows, with one seed.
We compared the area above final loss with performance on 55 GIFT-Eval tasks.
Across all eleven generators, Spearman correlation with CRPS relative to
seasonal naive is $0.00$ $[-0.89,\,+0.61]$. Excluding Chaotic and Waveform,
it is $-0.82$ for both CRPS and MASE, consistent with the hypothesis. We chose
this subset after seeing the results (Figure~\ref{fig:s2}; see
Figure~\ref{fig:s2curves} for learning curves). The correlation with the
CRPS ratio is positive after the first $0.4$ million windows, at
$+0.61$ $[+0.10,\,+0.88]$, and crosses zero by two million
(Figure~\ref{fig:s2corr}).

\section{Research Directions}

\paragraph{Epiplexity under other losses.} Both coding schemes permit training
with any shared reproducible update rule, including rules derived from other
losses~\citep{qiu2026}. Prequential coding additionally measures NLL before
the update. In requential coding, the student trains on teacher samples and
the estimate includes KL and coding overhead. Both schemes require specified
probability distributions. For a quantile model, specify interpolation,
tails, and forecast factorization to construct these distributions.

Area under the original loss remains a separate research question.
For a proper score $R_s(P,q)=H_s(P)+D_s(P,q)$~\citep{gneiting2007},
the area above the final risk is $\sum_i D_s(P,q_i)-N D_s(P,q_f)$.
For negative ELBO it also includes the change in the variational gap.
\citet{kingma2023} study how diffusion loss weights relate to ELBO.
\citet{xu2026} estimate the optimal denoising loss. The article in
\path{publications/article/} includes a brief treatment and a counterexample
to CRPS and KL inducing the same ordering.

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Research question.} When does area under the original loss
agree with a coding estimate and preserve the ranking of data sources?
How can we compute probabilities accurately and cheaply for quantile and
diffusion models?

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Candidate formulations.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Quantile loss. Summing quantile losses over a grid of levels approximates
  CRPS. CRPS is a strictly proper scoring rule whose divergence is the
  integrated squared difference between cumulative distributions. Define area
  as cumulative divergence, determine when it ranks sources like KL, and
  measure the approximation error from building a density from quantiles
  and computing log loss.
\item Diffusion loss. Specify a probabilistic model and a way to estimate its
  likelihood or variational code length. Determine how noise weights and the
  variational gap affect area and test a requential estimate for teacher and
  student at equal forecasting accuracy.
\item General case. A proper score defines a divergence and the final endpoint
  adds a correction to its cumulative sum. Find conditions on distribution
  families and learning trajectories under which different scores rank
  data sources in the same order.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Relevance to the paper.} Leading time-series models use
quantile loss (Chronos-2, TiRex, Moirai 2.0) or diffusion and flow matching
(Sundial). Once the required probabilities are defined, coding estimates
can apply to those training procedures. The relationship to the original
loss and the stability of source rankings need further study and are
relevant beyond time series.
\end{framed}

\paragraph{Effects of generators and training recipes.} Determine whether
and in what sense increasing generator complexity raises epiplexity.
Test how the area depends on learning rate, schedule, and initialization,
and whether generator rankings survive changes to the training recipe.

\paragraph{Thickened manifolds.} Convolve a distribution on a manifold
with Gaussian noise of width $\sigma$. This specifies structure and noise
by construction, with known entropy and Bayesian learning curve. The
setting isolates the measure on continuous data and yields an $S(\sigma)$ curve.
